# Developing Character: Building Individuality, Taste, and Depth

## Core idea

The goal is **not** to become contrarian, pretentious, or artificially "cultured."

The goal is to become a person who:

- has genuinely experienced different things
- knows what he likes and dislikes
- can explain **why**
- forms opinions independently rather than inheriting them from friends or popular culture
- remains curious about things outside his usual interests
- can appreciate something even when he does not personally like it
- is willing to revise his opinions after gaining new experiences

**Character = experience + reflection + independent judgment + principles.**

---

# 1. Don't just have preferences. Understand them.

Instead of:

> "I liked that movie."

Ask:

- What exactly did I like?
- What made it enjoyable?
- What emotion did it create?
- What was unusual about it?
- What did I dislike?
- Why did I dislike it?
- Does this reveal something about my taste?

### Example

Weak:

> "I liked *Physical: 100*. It was awesome."

Better:

> "I like *Physical: 100* because the competition is based heavily on physical capability rather than manufactured drama."

Deeper:

> "I initially liked the strength aspect, but I realized I actually find the strategic adaptation more interesting. Someone can be extraordinarily strong and still lose because the task doesn't reward their particular strength."

The progression is:

**Preference → Reason → Observation → Self-understanding**

---

# 2. Keep a Taste Journal

Whenever I experience something meaningful, record:

### What did I experience?

What was it?

### What did I like?

Be specific.

### What didn't I like?

Be specific.

### Why?

Try to identify the underlying reason.

### What did it make me think about?

Connect it to ideas, experiences, or values.

### What did I learn about my own taste?

This is the most important question.

# 3. Don't be afraid to dislike something

The goal is **not** to force myself to like sophisticated things.

Bad approach:

> "Tarkovsky is considered great, therefore I must like Tarkovsky."

Better approach:

> "Tarkovsky is considered important, so I'll give him a genuine chance. Then I'll figure out what I personally respond to."

I can say:

> "I understand why this is considered excellent, but it isn't for me."

That is much more valuable than pretending.

### Remember

**Appreciation ≠ personal preference.**

I can recognize that something is excellent without enjoying it.

---

# 4. Assume there is something worth discovering

When I encounter an unfamiliar genre, art form, hobby, or subject:

Don't begin with:

> "I don't like this."

Begin with:

> **"Millions of people find value in this. What am I not seeing yet?"**

My job is to find the interesting part.

Only after understanding it should I decide whether I actually like it.

This prevents shallow prejudice based on:

- stereotypes
- friends' opinions
- internet culture
- popularity
- social identity
- preconceived ideas

---

# 5. Explore from the best examples

When entering an unfamiliar field, don't begin randomly.

Start with works that are:

- historically important
- highly regarded
- influential
- considered among the best examples of the form

Then develop outward.

### Example

If exploring classical music:

**Bach → Mozart → Beethoven → Chopin → Debussy → Stravinsky**

If exploring cinema:

Start with highly regarded classics and influential directors before deciding that "old movies are boring."

The purpose is not to become an expert.

The purpose is to develop a **reference point**.

---

# 6. Follow curiosity chains

Don't consume random "cultured" material just to appear interesting.

Follow genuine curiosity.

Example:

> I like Christopher Nolan.

↓

> Why?

↓

> I realize I like nonlinear storytelling.

↓

> What other films use nonlinear storytelling?

↓

> I discover *Rashomon*.

↓

> I become interested in unreliable narration.

↓

> I explore that idea in literature.

This creates a **network of interests**.

It is much more valuable than randomly collecting cultural trivia.

---

# 7. Explore horizontally

Don't stay entirely inside my existing interests.

If I like:

- science → explore history/philosophy/art
- gym → explore martial arts/dance/climbing
- anime → explore cinema/literature/animation history
- physics → explore philosophy of science
- music → explore genres I normally dismiss

The purpose is to become a more **well-rounded human being**, not merely a more specialized version of myself.

---

# 8. Develop opinions about life, not just entertainment

Depth isn't only:

> "I like German films."

I should also think about:

### Values

- What do I respect in people?
- What do I consider courage?
- What do I consider integrity?
- What kind of behavior do I admire?

### Relationships

- What makes someone a good friend?
- What makes someone trustworthy?
- What kind of people do I want around me?

### Work

- What kind of work feels meaningful?
- What do I value more: freedom, achievement, stability, mastery, impact?
- What kind of person do I want to become professionally?

### Life

- What makes a life meaningful?
- What experiences do I want before I die?
- What am I unwilling to compromise on?
- What do I want my life to be about?

I don't need final answers.

I need to **think about the questions**.

---

# 9. Don't become contrarian for the sake of being different

Having character does **not** mean disagreeing with everyone.

Bad:

> "Everyone likes it, so I hate it."

That is still allowing everyone else to determine my opinion.

True independence is:

> "I don't care whether this is popular. I will decide what I think after experiencing it myself."

I can agree with the majority and still have an independent opinion.

---

# 10. Changing my mind is not weakness

A strong opinion should be capable of surviving examination.

But if new evidence or experience changes my view:

**Change it.**

The goal isn't:

> "Never change my opinion."

The goal is:

> **"My opinions should belong to me."**

Changing my mind because I learned something is intellectual growth.

Changing my mind because everyone else changed theirs is conformity.

---

# 11. Build experiences, not just knowledge

Character comes from living, not merely consuming information.

Regularly try things that are outside my normal routine:

- unfamiliar food
- new sports
- martial arts
- hiking
- museums
- galleries
- theatre
- concerts
- different kinds of films
- different genres of books
- cooking
- drawing
- photography
- travelling
- lectures
- historical places
- learning an instrument
- talking to people with very different backgrounds

The question afterward is:

> **"What did this experience teach me about myself?"**

---

# 12. A weekly system

Every week:

### 1. One unfamiliar movie

Watch something outside my normal taste.

Write a short reflection afterward.

### 2. One unfamiliar piece of music

Actually listen rather than using it as background noise.

Ask what I notice.

### 3. Read 30–60 pages

Preferably something outside my normal field.

### 4. One new experience

Do something I normally wouldn't do.

### 5. One reflection

Write:

> What did I discover about myself this week?

---

# 13. Monthly review

At the end of every month, answer:

### My tastes

- What did I discover that I genuinely like?
- What did I discover that I dislike?
- Did any opinion change?

### My interests

- What subjects am I becoming curious about?
- What rabbit holes did I fall into?

### My experiences

- What did I do that was genuinely new?
- What experience would I repeat?

### My character

- Did I express my opinions honestly?
- Did I merely follow the group?
- Did I avoid something because of a stereotype?
- Did I change my mind because of evidence or social pressure?

### My next month

Choose:

- 1 new artistic field
- 1 new book
- 2–4 films
- 1 new activity
- 1 topic to investigate deeply

---

# 14. The rule to remember

When I encounter something new:

> **Experience it → Understand it → Reflect on it → Decide what I think → Explain why.**

Not:

> **Someone else likes it → I like it too.**

And not:

> **Everyone likes it → I hate it because I'm different.**

Both are forms of dependence.

The objective is **independent judgment**.

---

# 15. What "developing character" ultimately means

I don't need to become an intellectual snob.

I don't need obscure hobbies.

I don't need to know every famous artist, director, author, or musician.

I don't need to deliberately become "different."

I need to **live deliberately**.

I want to reach a point where my interests, tastes, values, experiences, and opinions are recognizably **mine**.

When someone asks:

> "Why do you like this?"

I should be able to give a genuine answer.

When someone disagrees:

> I should be able to explain my position without becoming defensive.

When I encounter something unfamiliar:

> I should be curious before being dismissive.

When I discover that I was wrong:

> I should be willing to change my mind.

That is depth.

That is individuality.

That is character.
